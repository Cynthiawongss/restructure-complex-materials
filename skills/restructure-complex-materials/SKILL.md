# Classify and Restructure Complex Materials

Take material already collected or organized by someone else, understand its existing structure, and reorganize it around the current users' tasks. A structure that makes sense to its original organizer may be unclear to someone taking it over. Difficulty understanding material does not make it worthless. Preserve useful information and make its meaning, relationships, and entry points clear.

| Term | Who it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| User | The person requesting the task | Describing task instructions |
| Intended users | People who will find, understand, and use the resulting collection | Assessing whether the structure works |
| Original organizer | The person who previously collected or arranged the material | Explaining existing categories and conventions |

The user and the intended user may be the same person. Check structural clarity and content completeness separately.

## Inventory before restructuring

Read supplied or authorized material. Identify each source's actual content type, intended users, existing categories, major fields, and relationship to other versions. Recover the original organizing principle and identify conventions that may only be obvious to the original organizer. Check titles and descriptions against the content: a product proposal is not evidence of a live product, and an old version or verification worksheet is not necessarily the right everyday entry point.

At a level appropriate to the task, record sources, locations or record IDs, purposes, retention value, overlaps, gaps, and conflicts. A short explanation may suffice for a small collection; do not force it into a large inventory table.

- Judge importance against the intended users' current task and explain why. Suggest archiving temporarily irrelevant material rather than treating incomprehension as a reason to delete it.
- Distinguish exact duplicates, partial overlap, complementary content, old versions, and conflicts. Repetition does not imply lack of value.
- Look up unfamiliar field definitions; mark unresolved meanings as unknown instead of guessing from codes.
- If coverage is incomplete, state what was read. Do not claim unread content is absent or fully preserved.

## Organize around the intended users' tasks

Determine what decisions or actions the material needs to support. Reuse useful parts of the existing structure and explain what needs to change. Establish the overall structure before detailed reorganization. If the user only requests an outline, do not proceed to rewriting all content.

| Dimension | Question it answers | Examples and rules |
|---|---|---|
| Primary categories | Where can I find the content? | Tools by business purpose, or project files by delivery stage. Choose the axis for the task; do not impose one universal taxonomy |
| Attribute fields | How can I filter further? | Technology, platform, cost, owner, and status. Do not mix these into the primary categories |
| Process relationships | What comes first or next? | Provide a separate workflow when needed; sequence is different from category membership |
| Usage roles | What does each role need to see? | Business, execution, and maintenance may need different entry points. Use files, tables, or views as needed without duplicating all data |

Use a consistent classification basis at each level, with clear parent-child relationships. For items spanning categories, use a primary entry point with tags or cross-references rather than distorting the material to force a tree.

Define category boundaries briefly and include representative examples. Retain original names or codes in traceable fields while using meaningful labels in navigation. Explain codes when necessary.

When there are too many fields, assign three destinations around the task: show fields needed for finding, comparing, or acting in the main entry point; put long explanations and infrequent fields in expandable detail or related records; retain original field names, values, and sources in a traceability layer. Hiding or moving a field is not deletion. Before combining synonyms, confirm that meaning, units, and scope match and record the old-to-new field mapping. Do not merge owners, contacts, and approvers simply because they all contain names. Define key fields with examples without imposing a fixed column count.

## Preserve detail and source relationships

Provide an overview and a suggested navigation sequence before expanding details. Do not reduce everything to names and short summaries at the cost of objectives, steps, limitations, sources, or existing subcategories.

Maintain a mapping from original records or passages to new locations and dispositions: retained, merged, archived, or pending a decision. Preserve complementary fields when merging. Keep conflicting values and their sources instead of choosing silently. Matching item counts do not establish field-level completeness.

Mark project relationships, prerequisites, or owners absent from the sources as “not specified.” Label proposed additions as new suggestions. Do not attribute inferred relationships to the original organizer.

If the user requests analysis or a proposal only, deliver that scope. Permission to organize does not imply permission to delete originals. For authorized updates, write within scope, read back the result, and preserve originals and version relationships as instructed.

## Choose a usable presentation

Respect the user's chosen document, table, or mind-map format. Distinguish files, data tables, and different views of the same data. Do not assume each category needs its own file or table.

For mind maps, give independently expandable subcategories their own child nodes; line breaks within a node do not create a hierarchy. Let content determine depth rather than forcing four levels. When further editing matters, distinguish native nodes, editable map files, and display-only images.

For tables, arrange visible fields and navigation around the task. Address long text, column widths, row heights, and unexplained codes. If the tool cannot display everything, explain the limitation and propose suitable options. Do not silently switch formats, delete long content, or claim a visual check that has not happened.

Apply preferences such as left-to-right layout, colored tags, or one entry point per category when the user has expressed them. They are presentation choices for the task, not universal requirements.

## Deliver and verify

A typical result includes the material's nature and coverage, recommended entry points, the revised classification, key field definitions, and source mappings or unresolved issues. Scale this to the collection; do not create multiple files for a simple task.

Before delivery, check separately:

| Check | Passing criterion |
|---|---|
| Structure | Categories at each level have a clear basis; intended users understand where an item belongs and why |
| Completeness | Original content has a destination, complementary fields are retained, and conflicts are exposed |
| Factual grounding | Source content, inferences, and suggestions are distinguishable |
| Usability | Users can move from overview to detail and complete a lookup or comparison without unexplained abbreviations or excessive cell-by-cell clicking |
| Completion status | Report proposal, file-generation, write, content-check, and display-check status separately, without an unsupported claim that everything is finished |

## Example requests

“Use restructure-complex-materials on this tool collection someone else organized. Explain its existing structure and duplicates, then reorganize it by business purpose. Keep technology and platform as attributes and retain the detailed content. Provide a proposal only.”

“Restructure these project handover materials for business and maintenance staff. Preserve sources and complementary information, surface conflicts, and do not invent project relationships absent from the source material.”
